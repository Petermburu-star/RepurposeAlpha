RepurposeAlpha

How It Works

A decision support tool for drug repurposing. Type a disease, get a portfolio recommendation — with eight features covering the full lifecycle from discovery to pricing.

The Base Pipeline

When you type a disease name and click Analyze, four things happen in sequence:

  1. Discovery — The tool asks Open Targets (a public database) "which drugs have been tested for this disease?" It returns a list of candidates.
  2. Correlation — For each pair of candidates, it computes how biologically similar they are — shared targets, chemical structure, mechanism of action.
  3. Returns — For each candidate, it estimates expected payoff using phase, success rate, and number of competitors.
  4. Optimization — With correlations and returns, it runs a Markowitz calculation to rank candidates by risk-adjusted outcome.

The Eight Features

1

Portfolio Optimization

"What should I test?"

Treats candidate drugs like stocks. Each has an expected return (how promising) and volatility (how uncertain). Two drugs that are highly correlated are "the same bet" — holding both wastes money.

What it produces

Two portfolios — Max Sharpe (best return per unit of risk) and Min Volatility (safest bets).

What a scientist does with it

"The tool says test Cannabidiol first, and skip Cannabidivarin — they're 50% correlated, so I'd be repeating the same experiment."

Honest limit: Return estimates are phase-based, not drug-specific. Two Phase 3 drugs get identical expected returns.
2

Real Options Valuation

"When should I kill it?"

Every drug project has decision points — end of Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3. At each gate, you can continue or quit. The ability to quit is worth real money.

What it produces

A probability tree. At each gate, it asks: given the remaining cost and probability of success, is continuing worth it?

What a scientist does with it

"The tool says don't start this PWS project — DCF is -$137M. But if you're already in Phase 2 (someone paid the early costs), continue."

Honest limit: Uses average industry phase costs and success rates. Real numbers vary by disease and drug class.
3

Freedom-to-Operate

"Can I sell it?"

A repurposed drug is only valuable if you can protect it commercially. If the patent expired 20 years ago and it's generic, anyone can sell it — so nobody invests to develop it.

What it produces

Checks whether each candidate is already generic, still protected, or a biologic. Flags: high risk / medium / low.

What a scientist does with it

"Half my coronary artery candidates are generic. Even if one works, I can't make money on it. But the PWS candidates are mostly protected."

Honest limit: Uses a curated list of known generics, not real patent data. This is a screening signal, not legal advice.
4

Adaptive Trial Design

"How should I test it?"

You can test 5 drugs in 5 separate trials (slow, expensive) or in one shared trial with a common control group (faster, cheaper). Platform trials save money by sharing infrastructure.

What it produces

Compares three architectures: Fixed (N independent RCTs), Bayesian adaptive (shared control), Platform (multi-arm, add/drop).

What a scientist does with it

"The tool says I can save $22M and 40% of patients with a platform trial instead of 6 separate trials."

Honest limit: Uses standard industry assumptions (effect size, enrollment rate). Real trials vary.
5

Cross-Disease Mechanism Discovery

"What else does it treat?"

A drug tested for malaria might also be in trials for cancer, lupus, and COVID. That tells you two things: it has accumulated safety data, and other scientists believe in its repurposing potential.

What it produces

For each candidate, a count and list of other diseases it's being tested in.

What a scientist does with it

"Artesunate has 15 other indications. That's a lot of accumulated evidence — lower regulatory risk if it works for malaria too."

Honest limit: Doesn't tell you whether those other trials succeeded — just that they exist.
6

Negative Results Intelligence

"Has it already failed?"

Trials that fail are often never published. So researchers repeat failed experiments. ClinicalTrials.gov holds terminated trials, but nobody surfaces them systematically.

What it produces

Queries ClinicalTrials.gov for terminated trials, then distinguishes failure types:
Futility — the science didn't work → skip
Safety — the drug is dangerous → skip
Enrollment — logistics failed, not the drug → consider retrying
Business — the sponsor gave up, not the science → consider retrying

What a scientist does with it

"Primaquine has 7 prior failures for malaria — but 6 are 'unknown'. Meanwhile Acetaminophen has 7 failures too, but 0 for malaria."

Honest limit: Most terminated trials don't state why. "Unknown" is honest.
7

Combination Synergy

"What pairs well with it?"

Some drugs work better together than alone. Two drugs that hit different parts of the same pathway often synergize. Two drugs that hit the same target are redundant.

What it produces

Scores every pair of candidates on a synergy scale. Very low correlation = unrelated mechanisms. Moderate (0.1-0.5) = ideal pair. High (>0.8) = redundant.

What a scientist does with it

"The tool flagged Sulfadiazine + Trimethoprim as a top pair. Those two are sold together as co-trimoxazole — so the tool is correct."

Honest limit: No real synergy data — heuristic based on correlation and prior trial presence.
8

Cost-Based Pricing

"What price works?"

If you develop a repurposed drug, you need to charge enough per patient to recover costs. For a common disease, that's ~$30/patient/year. For a rare disease with 2,000 patients, it's ~$50,000/patient/year.

What it produces

Sustainable price per patient per year, and comparison to market reference prices (rare disease ~$150K, NTD ~$500, common ~$5K).

What a scientist does with it

"The tool says I need $32/patient/year for Primaquine in malaria. That's 94% below the reference NTD price — huge headroom. Viable."

Honest limit: Uses category-based pricing references, not disease-specific market data.

The Big Picture

RepurposeAlpha is a decision support tool for drug repurposing.

A scientist types a disease. The tool finds candidate drugs, scores them by portfolio return, tells you which to kill, whether you can protect them, how to test them, what else they treat, whether they've failed before, what pairs well, and what price works.

Before RepurposeAlpha: a scientist manually reviews 30 candidates in Excel and makes a gut decision.

After RepurposeAlpha: the tool produces a defensible recommendation with sources for every number, in about 60 seconds.
